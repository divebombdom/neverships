Fake delivery texts and emails: how the scam works and how to avoid it

Why 'we missed your delivery' messages are so convincing, the warning signs to look for, and what to do if you clicked.

By the NeverShips team · Updated November 8, 2026 · 3 min read

"Your package could not be delivered. Update your address here." If you've received a message like this, you're not alone. Fake delivery notifications are one of the most widespread phishing scams, and they work because most of us really are waiting for a package.

How the scam works

The message, usually a text but sometimes an email, claims to be from a well-known carrier or retailer. It says there's a problem: a missed delivery, an unpaid fee, an incomplete address. It includes a link. The link leads to a convincing copy of the carrier's website that asks you to "confirm your details" or pay a small redelivery fee, often just a dollar or two.

The small fee is the trick. It feels too minor to worry about, but the real goal is your full card number, expiration date, and security code, plus your name and address. Some versions also try to collect login details or install malware.

Warning signs

  • You didn't sign up for texts from that carrier. Carriers generally only text you if you opted in with your tracking number.
  • No tracking number, or one that doesn't match any order.
  • A strange link. The domain isn't the carrier's real site, uses odd characters, or is a link shortener.
  • Urgency and small fees. "Pay within 24 hours or your package will be returned."
  • Sent from an ordinary phone number or an email address that isn't on the carrier's domain.
  • Generic greeting and slightly off grammar or formatting.

The safe way to check a delivery

Never use the link in the message. Instead:

  1. Open the order confirmation email from the store you actually bought from.
  2. Copy the tracking number from there.
  3. Type the carrier's website address yourself, or use its official app, and paste the number in.

If there's a real problem, the official tracking page will show it.

If you clicked the link

Clicking alone usually isn't a disaster, but don't enter anything. Close the page. If you're worried, run your phone's security updates and avoid installing anything the page prompts you to install.

If you entered your details

  1. Call your card issuer right away using the number on the back of your card. Tell them your card details were phished and ask for a new card.
  2. Watch your statements for unfamiliar charges, including small test charges.
  3. Change passwords if you entered any login information, and turn on two-factor authentication.
  4. Report it. In the US you can forward scam texts to 7726 (SPAM) and report fraud to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Most carriers also have a page for reporting phishing.

Reduce how many you get

  • Use your phone's built-in spam filtering and "filter unknown senders" option.
  • Sign up for official delivery notifications directly with carriers, so real alerts come through the carrier's app.
  • Be careful where you share your phone number, especially on unfamiliar shopping sites.
