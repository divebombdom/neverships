How to spot a fake tech deal before you pay

Seven red flags that a too-good-to-be-true phone, laptop, or GPU listing is exactly that, plus a two-minute check you can run on any store.

By the NeverShips team · Updated November 8, 2026 · 3 min read

Fake tech deals are one of the most common online shopping scams, and they work because the products are expensive and the discounts feel plausible. A graphics card that normally costs a month's rent suddenly shows up at half price, and the part of your brain that wants it starts making excuses. Here's how to slow down and check before you hand over card details.

1. The price is far below every major retailer

Real discounts on current-generation tech are usually modest: 10 to 25 percent during big sales, sometimes more on older models being cleared out. A brand-new flagship phone or a just-released console at 60 or 70 percent off from a store you've never heard of is almost never real. Look up the same exact model number at two or three large retailers. If one unknown site is dramatically cheaper than all of them, that gap is the warning.

2. The website address is new, odd, or almost right

Scam storefronts often use domains that look like a known brand with an extra word ("-outlet", "-deals", "-official"), a swapped letter, or an unusual ending. Many are only a few weeks old because they get reported and shut down quickly. A free WHOIS lookup shows when a domain was registered. A store claiming "20 years in business" on a domain created last month is telling you something.

3. Payment only by wire, gift card, crypto, or payment app

This is the single biggest red flag. Credit cards give you the strongest protection, because you can dispute a charge for goods you never received. Wire transfers, gift cards, cryptocurrency, and friends-and-family payment app transfers are very hard to reverse. If a store "has a problem with card payments today" and asks you to pay another way, stop.

4. No real way to contact anyone

Legitimate retailers list a physical address, a working phone number or live chat, and a support email on their own domain. Scam sites often have only a contact form, a free email address, or an address that turns out to be a residential home or a shipping depot when you search it.

5. Pressure everywhere

Countdown timers that reset when you refresh, "only 2 left" banners on every product, and pop-ups saying someone in your city just bought one are all designed to stop you from thinking. Real stores use urgency too, but if every element on the page is shouting at you, treat it as a tactic, not information. (Yes, NeverShips has a deal timer. Ours is a joke, and it rotates every 75 seconds on purpose.)

6. Reviews that all sound the same

Hundreds of five-star reviews posted in the same week, with similar wording and no photos, are a sign of fake reviews. Reviews hosted only on the store's own site can't be trusted on their own. Search the store's name plus "reviews" or "scam" and look at what independent sites and forums say.

7. A vague or missing return policy

Established stores spell out how long you have to return an item, who pays return shipping, and how refunds work. Scam stores either skip this entirely or copy a generic policy that mentions a different company's name. Read it. A copy-paste mistake is a giveaway.

The two-minute check

Before paying an unfamiliar store, run through this quickly:

  1. Search the exact model number at two big retailers and compare prices.
  2. Look up the domain's registration date.
  3. Search the store name plus "scam" and "reviews."
  4. Confirm you can pay by credit card through a normal checkout.
  5. Read the return policy and look for a real address.

If two or more of these fail, walk away. A real deal will still exist at a store you trust, maybe a little less exciting, but it will actually arrive. For a deeper walkthrough, see how to check if an online store is legit.

If you already paid

Act quickly. Contact your card issuer or bank, explain that you suspect fraud, and ask about a dispute or chargeback. Save screenshots of the listing, your order confirmation, and any emails. In the US you can also report the site to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Change any password you reused on the scam site.
