Are extended warranties on electronics worth it?

How extended warranties and protection plans work, the coverage you may already have, and a simple way to decide.

By the NeverShips team · Updated November 8, 2026 · 3 min read

At checkout, you'll almost always be offered an extended warranty or protection plan. Retailers push them hard because they're profitable. That doesn't mean they're always a bad idea, but it does mean you should decide calmly, not in the last ten seconds before paying. Know what you already have

  • The manufacturer's warranty: most new electronics come with a limited warranty, often one year, covering defects. It typically doesn't cover drops, spills, or normal wear.
  • Your credit card: some credit cards extend the manufacturer's warranty by up to a year on purchases made with the card. Check your card's benefits guide; terms vary widely.
  • Consumer law: depending on where you live, consumer protection laws may require products to last a reasonable amount of time regardless of the stated warranty.
  • Home or renters insurance: may cover theft or certain damage, subject to your deductible.

Extended warranty vs accidental damage protection

These are often bundled but are different:

  • Extended warranty: keeps covering defects after the manufacturer's warranty ends.
  • Accidental damage protection: covers cracked screens, drops, and spills, usually with a per-claim fee or deductible.

For phones and laptops that leave the house, accidental damage is far more common than defects. A plan that only extends defect coverage may not cover the thing most likely to happen.

When a plan can make sense

  • Expensive, portable devices you carry daily, like phones and laptops, especially with accidental damage coverage.
  • Devices with costly, hard-to-repair parts, where one repair could approach the plan's price.
  • If a single unexpected repair bill would be a real hardship for you.
  • Plans from the manufacturer, which often include authorized repairs with genuine parts.

When to skip it

  • Inexpensive items where a replacement costs about the same as the plan.
  • Devices that mostly stay at home, like TVs and desktop monitors, where failures tend to show up early (inside the standard warranty) or not at all.
  • When your credit card already extends the warranty.
  • Plans with high deductibles, many exclusions, or claims handled by an unfamiliar third party.

A quick way to decide

  1. Find the plan's total cost, including any per-claim fee.
  2. Look up what the most likely repair would cost without it, such as a screen replacement.
  3. Ask honestly how likely that repair is for you.
  4. Check the fine print: who handles claims, what's excluded, whether repairs use genuine parts, and whether you can cancel for a refund.

Many retailers let you add a plan within a set window after purchase, so you don't have to decide at checkout. Take the receipt home, read the terms, and choose with a clear head.

The alternative is self-insuring: put the money you'd spend on plans into a savings pot for repairs. Over many purchases, most people come out ahead this way, as long as one big repair wouldn't cause trouble.
